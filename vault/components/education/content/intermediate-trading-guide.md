You already know what a trend is and what support and resistance look like\. This module goes further\. It covers the indicators, patterns, and signals that intermediate traders use to sharpen entry timing, identify exhaustion points, and avoid the traps that catch traders who read charts but not deeply enough\.

## Momentum Indicators

Momentum indicators measure the rate of price change rather than direction alone\. They tell you whether a trend is strengthening or losing steam, often before the price itself signals a reversal\.

__Relative Strength Index \(RSI\)__

RSI measures the speed and magnitude of recent price changes on a scale of 0 to 100\. Traditionally, readings above 70 indicate overbought conditions, and readings below 30 indicate oversold conditions\. But the real power of RSI for intermediate traders is not in those threshold levels\. It is in divergence\.

__RSI Signal__

__What It Looks Like__

__What It Means__

Bullish Divergence

Price makes a lower low, but RSI makes a higher low

Selling pressure is weakening even as prices decline\. A reversal or bounce may be approaching\.

Bearish Divergence

Price makes a higher high, but RSI makes a lower high

Buying momentum is fading even as the price rises\. The rally may be running out of energy\.

Overbought \(>70\) in uptrend

RSI sustained above 70 across multiple readings

Strong momentum, not necessarily a reversal signal on its own\. Look for divergence before acting\.

Oversold \(<30\) in downtrend

RSI sustained below 30 across multiple readings

Strong downward momentum\. In live sports markets, a significant negative game event often follows\.

On InPlay's performance index, RSI divergence is particularly useful during extended momentum runs\. After a team has been executing well for two or three consecutive possessions, the price will often overshoot fair value\. RSI bearish divergence in that context is a signal to consider taking partial profits or tightening your stop rather than adding exposure\.

__Moving Average Convergence Divergence \(MACD\)__

MACD uses two exponential moving averages \(typically 12\-period and 26\-period\) and plots the difference between them, along with a signal line \(typically a 9\-period EMA of that difference\)\. When the MACD line crosses above the signal line, it is a bullish signal\. When it crosses below, it is bearish\.

The MACD histogram, which shows the distance between the MACD and signal lines, is particularly useful\. A histogram that shrinks even while the price is still moving in one direction is an early warning that momentum is decelerating\. This is the histogram divergence signal, and it often precedes the crossover by several price bars, giving you more reaction time\.

__InPlay Application__

In live game trading, MACD is more useful for confirming trend direction before a game than for real\-time decision\-making during one\. The index moves too fast in\-game for lagging indicators to be the primary driver\. Use MACD for pre\-game bias and RSI for in\-game timing\.

## Candlestick Patterns

Candlestick charts show four data points for each period: the open, the high, the low, and the close\. The body of the candle represents the range between the open and close prices\. The wicks represent the extremes hit during the period\. Learning to read candlestick patterns gives you a shorthand for interpreting market psychology at specific price levels\.

## Volume Analysis

Volume is one of the most underused tools in intermediate technical analysis\. The principle is simple: price moves on high volume are more meaningful than price moves on low volume\. But knowing how to interpret the relationship between price and volume in different contexts is where the real insight lives\.

## Chart Patterns That Matter

Beyond individual candlesticks, certain multi\-period price patterns carry predictive weight\. These are formations that reflect the psychological battle between buyers and sellers playing out over multiple trading periods\.

__Head and Shoulders__

A bearish reversal pattern\. Three peaks, with the middle peak \(the head\) higher than the two outer peaks \(shoulders\)\. Breaking below the neckline connecting the two troughs signals a likely reversal of the downward trend\.

__Double Top / Double Bottom__

Two peaks at roughly the same level \(top\) or two troughs at roughly the same level \(bottom\)\. The price tests a level twice without breaking through, signaling a likely reversal\. The more time between the two touches, the stronger the signal\.

__Bull Flag / Bear Flag__

A sharp move in one direction, followed by a brief consolidation that runs counter to the trend\. The consolidation is the flag\. When price breaks out of the flag in the original direction, it often continues with renewed momentum\. Common in live sports markets after major game events\.

__Wedge__

Converging trend lines that squeeze price into a tighter range\. A rising wedge is bearish \(buyers losing strength despite higher prices\)\. A falling wedge is bullish\. The breakout direction signals the next significant move\.

__The Key Principle at This Level__

Individual technical signals are not enough\. The intermediate trader looks for confluence: multiple signals aligning at the same price level or at the same moment\. RSI divergence at a resistance level, confirmed by a bearish candlestick pattern and declining volume, is a materially stronger signal than any one of those three alone\.

__ADVANCED ORDER MANAGEMENT__

__VIDEO__

Most beginners think about orders as a binary: you are either in or out\. Advanced order management treats entry and exit as processes rather than events\. Scaling, bracketing, and trailing stops are the tools that give you more precise control over risk and reward as a trade develops\.

## Scaling In: Building a Position in Stages

Rather than deploying your full intended position size at once, scaling in means entering in multiple tranches as the trade confirms your thesis\.

The logic: if you are not yet certain the trade is working, why take the full risk? Start with a smaller position\. If price moves in your favor and your thesis strengthens, add to the position\. Your average entry improves if you add at better prices, and your risk exposure grows only as confidence in the trade grows\.

__Approach__

__Entry Structure__

__When to Use__

Full Position Entry

Enter 100% of the intended size at once

High\-conviction setups where speed is critical and the entry level is clearly defined

Two\-Tranche Scale In

Enter 50% at the initial signal; add 50% on confirmation

When the setup looks good, but you want the price to confirm the direction before full commitment

Three\-Tranche Scale In

Enter 33% at signal; 33% on confirmation; 33% at breakout

Extended trade setups where you expect the move to develop over multiple periods

Aggressive Add

Enter small; add significantly on strong confirmation

When a thesis develops stronger than expected, and you want to capitalize on the opportunity

__The Risk of Averaging Down__

Scaling in is not the same as averaging down, and scaling in means adding to a position that is moving in your favor because your thesis is being confirmed\. Averaging down means adding to a losing position to reduce your average entry price\. One is a disciplined process\. The other is a psychological response to a trade going wrong\. Do not confuse them\.

## Scaling Out: Taking Profits Strategically

Selling your entire position at one price means you have to be exactly right about the exit level\. Scaling out means selling in tranches, capturing different portions of the move at different price levels\.

__1__

__Sell the first tranche at your initial profit target__

This locks in some profit and removes the fear of giving it all back\. Psychologically, realizing a partial gain makes it easier to hold the rest with patience\.

__2__

__Move your stop\-loss to break\-even on the remaining position__

Once you have taken partial profit, protect yourself from turning a winning trade into a loser on the remainder\. Moving the stop to entry eliminates that risk\.

__3__

__Let the second tranche run toward an extended target__

Now that you have locked in profit and protected downside, you can hold the remaining position with less anxiety and more patience\.

__4__

__Sell the final tranche at your extended target or on a reversal signal__

Do not hold indefinitely\. Define your final exit before the trade begins, or exit on a clear technical reversal signal\.

## Trailing Stops

A trailing stop moves with the price as it goes in your favor, but stays fixed when the price moves against you\. If you set a trailing stop 3\.00 points below the current price and the price rises from 50\.00 to 58\.00, your stop has moved from 47\.00 to 55\.00\. If the price then falls to 55\.00, you are stopped out, but you have locked in a 5\.00 per unit profit\.

Trailing stops solve one of the hardest problems in trading: knowing when to exit a winning position\. Rather than predicting the exact top, you let the market tell you when momentum has shifted\. The trailing stop automatically converts a moving profit into a locked gain\.

__Stop Type__

__How It Works__

__Best Situation__

Fixed Stop\-Loss

Set at a specific price; does not move

Clearly defined risk level before entry; short time\-horizon trades

Break\-Even Stop

Moved to entry price after partial profit taken

Protecting capital while allowing a winning portion to run

Trailing Stop

Moves with price in your favor; holds when price reverses

Extended momentum trades where you do not want to cap the upside

Time\-Based Exit

Close the position after a fixed period regardless of price

Live game trades where the game context changes at halftime or late in the fourth quarter

## Bracket Orders

A bracket order combines your entry, stop\-loss, and profit target into a single instruction placed at the time of entry\. You define all three parameters before the trade opens\. When the entry executes, the platform automatically holds both the stop\-loss and the profit\-target orders\. Whichever triggers first cancels the other\.

This is called a One\-Cancels\-Other \(OCO\) structure\. Bracket orders are the most disciplined way to trade in fast\-moving markets because they remove the need to place exit orders after you are in a position manually\. In a live sports market where momentum can shift in seconds, having your exits pre\-set is not just efficient\. It is often the difference between a planned outcome and a reactionary one\.

__The Discipline Advantage__

The traders who most consistently outperform in live markets are not the ones who are fastest at placing orders\. They are the ones who have already decided what they are doing before they need to\. Bracket orders enforce that discipline structurally\. They make it impossible to forget your stop\-loss or get greedy about your target at the moment of maximum emotional involvement\.

__READING MARKET MICROSTRUCTURE__

__VIDEO__

Most traders analyze charts\. Fewer traders analyze the market mechanics underneath the charts\. Market microstructure is the study of how trades are executed, how buyers and sellers interact at the order level, and what those interactions reveal about market participants' intentions\. Understanding it gives you an edge that chart reading alone cannot\.

## The Order Book

The order book is the live record of all pending buy and sell orders at every price level\. On the buy side \(bids\), orders are stacked from the current price downward\. On the sell side \(asks\), orders are stacked above the current price\. The depth of orders at each level tells you where meaningful support and resistance are in real time, not just historically\.

__Order Book Feature__

__What to Look For__

__Trading Implication__

Large bid cluster

Significant buy orders are stacked at a specific price below the current

Strong support level\. Sellers may struggle to push the price through this zone\.

Large ask cluster

Significant sell orders are stacked at a specific price above the current

Strong resistance level\. Buyers may struggle to push the price through without heavy volume\.

Thin book

Very few orders on either side

Low liquidity\. Price can move sharply on relatively small trades\. Exercise caution with position size\.

Book imbalance

Significantly more bids than asks, or vice versa

Signals near\-term directional pressure\. More bids than asks suggest buying pressure is dominant\.

## Bid\-Ask Spread Dynamics

You know the spread is the difference between the bid and ask prices\. The intermediate insight is that the spread is not static\. It widens and narrows based on conditions, and the dynamics of that movement tell you something about market confidence\.

__Tight Spread__

High liquidity, active participation, and reasonable price agreement\. Execution is efficient\. This is the best environment for trading\.

__Wide Spread__

Low liquidity or high uncertainty\. Market makers and participants are less confident in fair value\. Execution is expensive\. Reduce position size or wait for conditions to stabilize\.

__Spread Spike__

A sudden widening of the spread, often on a game event or breaking news\. Indicates that the market is rapidly repricing in response to new information\. Entering a spread spike means paying a high immediate cost\. Better to wait for the spread to normalize\.

In InPlay's live market, spread dynamics are directly tied to game events\. Pre\-kickoff spreads tend to be tighter as the market has established a stable price\. During a scoring drive or a major game event, spreads widen as participants rapidly reprice\. Recognizing this pattern helps you choose the right moments to enter and the moments to wait\.

## Price Levels and Market Memory

Markets have memory\. A price level that acted as resistance three weeks ago will often act as resistance again the first time the price approaches it, even without any conscious effort from any individual trader\. This is because many traders who sold at that level last time are watching for it again, and the accumulated effect of their individual actions recreates the dynamic\.

For InPlay traders, this manifests as recurring price levels on the Performance Security index for specific teams\. Because all participants trade through InPlay's single liquidity pool on tZERO's ATS, these price memory levels are shared across the entire market\. A team that has consistently traded between 42\.00 and 60\.00 across multiple games creates a range that experienced traders know and reference\. Buying near 42\.00 and selling near 60\.00 is not sophisticated analysis\. But recognizing when the market has deviated significantly from that range, and why, is where the real trading decision lives\.

## Reading Trade Flow During Live Games

Trade flow analysis in a live sports market is about reading the urgency and size of transactions as they happen\. When large orders execute in quick succession in one direction, it signals that informed or highly motivated participants are moving\. That is a different signal from a slow price drift driven by small orders over time\.

__Flow Signal__

__What It Indicates__

__How to Use It__

Aggressive buyers willing to pay the full ask price; strong conviction

Consider following the direction with a scaled\-in long, confirming it with your own analysis

Large sell orders executing at the bid

Aggressive sellers willing to sell at the bid; strong conviction to exit

Consider the same directional bias for a short, confirming with your own analysis

Alternating small orders in both directions

__The Microstructure Edge__

Most traders see price\. Fewer see what is happening underneath it\. When you understand why the spread is widening, what the order book depth suggests about real support levels, and what the flow of transactions reveals about other participants' intentions, you are operating with more information than most market participants\. In a competitive trading environment like the InPlay Trading Challenge, that additional context compounds into a genuine, sustainable edge\.

__POSITION SIZING AND THE KELLY CRITERION__

__VIDEO__

The beginner guide established the 1\-2% risk\-per\-trade rule as a simple, effective guardrail\. That rule is not wrong\. But at the intermediate level, position sizing becomes a more nuanced tool for optimizing long\-term growth\. The Kelly Criterion is the mathematical framework that underlies most serious approaches to position sizing\.

## The Kelly Criterion: The Theory

The Kelly Criterion, developed by John Kelly at Bell Labs in 1956, answers the question: given a specific edge and a specific payoff ratio, what fraction of your capital should you risk on each bet to maximize the long\-term growth rate of your account?

The formula is:

__Kelly % = W \- \(1 \- W\) / R__

Where W is your win rate \(the percentage of profitable trades\), and R is the win/loss ratio \(the average size of your winning trades divided by the average size of your losing trades\)\.

__Win/Loss Ratio \(R\)__

__Kelly %__

__Practical Interpretation__

1\.5:1

21\.7%

Theoretically optimal but very aggressive\. Real edge uncertainty means this is dangerously high\.

55%

2\.0:1

32\.5%

Even more aggressive\. Almost no professional uses anything close to this\. Kelly assumes perfect edge knowledge\.

60%

1\.5:1

33\.3%

Consistently profitable edge with strong payoffs\. Still far too aggressive for practical use\.

55%

8\.3%

More modest edge\. A fractional Kelly of around 2\-4% would be practical\.

## Why Full Kelly Is Too Aggressive

The Kelly Criterion assumes you know your exact win rate and exact payoff ratio with certainty\. In practice, you do not\. Your estimates of your own edge are based on a limited sample of historical trades\. If your true win rate is 50% but you estimate it at 55%, the Kelly formula will oversize your positions relative to your actual edge\. This creates a risk of ruin far higher than the theory suggests\.

The practical solution used by most serious traders is Fractional Kelly, typically half\-Kelly or quarter\-Kelly\. This means taking 50% or 25% of the full Kelly recommendation\. It sacrifices some theoretical growth rate in exchange for a significantly lower drawdown and greater robustness to edge\-estimation error\.

## Building Your Sizing Model

An intermediate trader does not need to apply pure Kelly mathematics to every trade\. What you do need is a systematic approach to varying position size based on the strength of your setup\. Not all setups are equal\. A high\-conviction setup with multiple confirming signals deserves more capital than a marginal one\.

__1__

__Define your setup quality tiers__

Label your setups: High Conviction, Standard, and Speculative\. Each tier gets a different maximum position size as a percentage of capital\.

__2__

__Set your base risk unit__

Your base unit might be 1% of capital on a Standard setup\. High Conviction gets 2%\. Speculative gets 0\.5%\.

__3__

__Adjust for the current account state__

If you are in a drawdown, reduce all sizes by 25\-50% until you return to peak\. Drawdowns are the wrong time to take on greater risk\.

__4__

__Never exceed your maximum__

No single trade justifies exceeding your maximum position size, regardless of how strong the setup looks\. Certainty is an illusion in trading\.

__5__

__Track actual vs expected outcomes by tier__

Over 30\-50 trades per tier, does your High Conviction tier actually outperform Standard? If not, your tier classification needs refinement\.

__The Compounding Effect of Sizing__

Position sizing is the variable with the most leverage over long\-term account growth\. A trader with a 55% win rate and excellent sizing will outperform a trader with a 60% win rate and poor sizing\. Getting the size right is not a detail\. It is one of the two or three most important decisions you make on every trade\.

## Running Positions Across Multiple Horizons

The power of InPlay's multi\-horizon structure is that you can express the same fundamental thesis across multiple timeframes, each reinforcing the other\. The risk is that you create unintended correlation or conflicting exposure without realizing it\.

__The Reinforcing Structure: Using Horizons Together__

Example: You have a strong season\-long thesis on the San Francisco 49ers\. You are long on their season position\. They have a favorable home game this week against a weak defense\. You also open a live game long before kickoff\. Your two positions are aligned\. If the 49ers perform well, both positions benefit\. If they underperform, both positions take a loss\. This is not necessarily a problem\. It is a deliberate decision to concentrate exposure around a conviction you hold\.

The key is that this concentration is intentional\. You have decided to size up your exposure to this thesis\. You have not accidentally doubled up\.

__The Hedging Structure: Using Horizons to Manage Risk__

A more sophisticated use of multiple horizons is deliberate hedging\. Example: You hold a season\-long long position on a team that has a particularly difficult game this week against a dominant defense\. You are not changing your season\-long thesis, but you want to protect yourself from a bad week that could damage your account\.

You open a live game short position for that specific match\. If the team underperforms in the game, your short captures the loss that would have hurt the unrealized value of your season position\. If they perform well, your season position gains and your live short loses, but the net effect is controlled\. This is not about prediction\. It is about managing risk around a known difficult event while maintaining your longer\-term conviction\.

__The Conflict to Avoid: Unintentional Internal Hedging__

The most common multi\-horizon mistake is holding a long on a team's season position and a short on their live position in the same game without a clear strategic reason\. This internal hedge partially cancels out both positions\. You are paying the spread on both sides of the same thesis\. Losses on the other offset your gains on one\. The net result is usually worse than simply holding one position and managing it properly\.

__The Rule__

Before adding a position at any horizon, ask: Does this reinforce my existing thesis, hedge a specific risk deliberately, or conflict with an existing position unintentionally? If the answer is the third option, do not add the position\.

## Using Pre\-Season Positioning as a Price Advantage

Pre\-season positions are opened before the market has fully priced a team's expected performance through their Performance Security\. This is where your fundamental research advantage is most valuable\. Sportradar's historical data gives you the same underlying metrics the market uses, but your interpretation of that data ahead of the season is where the edge comes from\. If you believe a team is undervalued before the broader market catches up, the pre\-season entry is the highest\-margin version of that thesis\.

The tradeoff is time\. Pre\-season positions sit in your portfolio for weeks or months\. They require patience and a willingness to hold through short\-term noise\. The discipline is in not converting a season\-long position into a live trade because a single game spooked you\. If your season thesis is intact, a bad week is not a reason to close the position\. It may be a reason to add to it\.

## Momentum Trading: Riding the Move

Momentum trading is the practice of entering a position in the direction of a strong, confirmed price move with the expectation that the move will continue\. The underlying logic is that price momentum tends to persist in the short term because market participants do not all react to the same information simultaneously\.

In InPlay's live market, momentum is particularly pronounced during sustained performance runs\. When a team converts three consecutive third downs, scores twice in a quarter, and its defense holds the opponent to three\-and\-outs, that level of performance tends to continue for at least a few more possessions before the game rebalances\. The traders who enter on the first clear signal of this momentum, rather than the fifth, are capturing the best risk/reward window\.

__Identifying High\-Probability Momentum Entry Points__

__1__

__Wait for a pullback after the initial move__

The first 60 seconds after a major game event are the most volatile and the least reliable entry window\. The price often overshoots and then partially retraces\. The pullback entry offers better value and confirms that momentum is continuing rather than exhausting\.

__2__

__Confirm with volume__

A momentum move supported by increasing trade volume is more reliable than one on thin volume\. Volume confirms that the move reflects genuine conviction, not just a few large orders\.

__3__

__Check the broader game context__

A team scoring a touchdown when down by 14 in the fourth quarter is a different signal from scoring when up by 7 in the second quarter\. The same price movement means different things in different game contexts\.

__4__

__Know your exit before you enter__

Momentum can reverse fast\. Your profit target and trailing stop need to be defined before the trade opens\. Chasing a momentum move without a clear exit plan leads to giving back gains when the momentum fades\.

## Mean Reversion: Fading the Overreaction

Mean reversion is the opposite of momentum trading\. Instead of following the move, you bet against an extreme move returning toward its average\. In sports markets, this works best when the market has clearly overreacted to an event that does not fundamentally alter the team's sustained performance\.

The classic InPlay mean reversion setup: a team throws an interception, the performance index drops sharply by 8\-10 points, but the team's overall game metrics are still strong, the QB's efficiency for the game is above average, and the score margin is still manageable\. The market has priced a single negative event as though it reflects a lasting performance decline\. It usually does not\. The mean reversion trade is the long entry into that dip\.

__When to Use Each Approach__

Use momentum trading when the game fundamentals are clearly shifting in one direction, and the move is early\. Use mean reversion when a single event creates a sharp, disproportionate price move against an otherwise healthy performance backdrop\. Never use both approaches on the same team in the same game without a very specific strategic rationale\.

It is a system of exposures that interact in ways that either amplify or dampen your overall risk\. Understanding correlation is the foundation of portfolio thinking\.

## What Is Correlation?

Correlation measures the degree to which two securities move in the same direction at the same time\. Correlation ranges from \-1\.0 to \+1\.0\.

__Correlation__

__Meaning__

__Portfolio Implication__

\+1\.0

Perfect positive correlation: both assets always move in the same direction by the same amount

Holding both provides no diversification\. You effectively have a single, larger position\.

\+0\.5 to \+0\.8

Strong positive correlation: both tend to move in the same direction

Partial diversification\. A bad outcome for one will likely hurt the other too, just not equally\.

0\.0

Zero correlation: movements are independent

True diversification\. The two positions do not influence each other\.

\-0\.5 to \-0\.8

Negative correlation: assets tend to move in opposite directions

Natural hedge\. Losses on one tend to be offset by gains on the other\.

\-1\.0

Perfect negative correlation: assets always move in opposite directions

Complete hedge\. Total neutralization of exposure\.

## Correlation Risks in InPlay's Market

Several types of correlation risk are specific to performance securities trading\. Understanding them prevents the illusion of diversification\.

__Same\-Game Correlation__

Two teams playing each other in the same game are negatively correlated by definition\. One team outperforming means the other is underperforming\. Holding long positions in the same game creates natural internal hedging, reducing net exposure\.

__Conference Correlation__

Teams in the same conference often share performance drivers: common opponents, shared weather conditions during the same week, and similar scheduling patterns\. Two AFC teams on a short week in cold weather carry correlated risk factors\.

__Macro Game\-Day Correlation__

When multiple NFL games are played on the same afternoon, external factors such as regional weather patterns or a particularly physical style of play across the league that week can create broad correlations among teams you would not expect to be linked\.

__Direction Correlation__

Holding all long positions across your portfolio means you are exposed to any single factor that affects all teams simultaneously, such as a rule change, a league\-wide officiating emphasis, or a shift in game conditions\.

## Building a Balanced Book

A balanced trading book is one where your positions are distributed across different teams, different game windows, and where possible, different directional biases\. A book with eight long positions in Sunday afternoon NFL games has high concentration risk\. Any factor that broadly affects that specific game window will hit every position simultaneously\.

## Variance vs Process Failure

Not all drawdowns are created equal\. The most important diagnostic question after a losing streak is: Were these losses the result of bad process or bad variance?

__Normal Variance__

You followed your process correctly\. Your setups were sound\. Your position sizing was correct\. Your stops were set\. The trades did not work out because of unpredictable game outcomes\. This is normal\. A 55% win rate means that 45% of correctly identified trades lose money\.

__Process Failure__

You deviated from your process\. You moved stop\-losses to avoid being stopped out\. You sized up on a gut feeling\. You ignored your research and traded on emotion\. These losses are not just financially damaging\. They are information telling you that your discipline has degraded under pressure\.

The response to variance is patience\. The response to process failure is a systematic return to discipline\. The difference is critical\. Treating a variance\-driven losing streak as a process failure leads to unnecessary changes to a working system\. Treating process failure as bad luck prevents the self\-correction that is needed\.

__GLOSSARY OF KEY TERMS__

Significantly more bid orders than ask orders \(or vice versa\) in the order book, indicating near\-term directional buying or selling pressure\.

A combined entry, stop\-loss, and profit target order is placed simultaneously\. The stop and target are One\-Cancels\-Other \(OCO\)\.

A performance metric that reflects current conditions in real time, such as live possession stats or current\-game efficiency\.

__Concentration Risk__

The risk of holding too many correlated positions, meaning a single adverse event affects multiple positions simultaneously\.

A statistical measure of how closely two securities move in relation to each other\. Ranges from \-1\.0 \(perfectly opposite\) to \+1\.0 \(perfectly aligned\)\.

Structured, intentional review and testing of trading performance to identify and improve specific capabilities\.

A systematic, repeatable advantage in approach that produces better\-than\-random outcomes over a statistically significant sample of trades\.

__Engulfing Pattern__

A reversal candlestick pattern where a large candle fully encompasses the body of the prior candle, signaling a potential change in trend direction\.

A moving average that gives more weight to recent price data than older data, making it more responsive to recent price changes\.

__Hammer__

A bullish reversal candlestick with a small body near the top and a long lower wick, indicating buyers recovered control after an intraperiod decline\.

__Head and Shoulders__

A bearish reversal chart pattern with three peaks: a higher middle peak \(head\) flanked by two lower peaks \(shoulders\)\.

__Histogram Divergence__

The MACD histogram shrinks while the price continues in one direction, signaling decelerating momentum before a potential reversal\.

__Internal Hedging__

Holding positions on the same security in opposite directions across different horizons, unintentionally offsetting gains and losses\.

__Kelly Criterion__

A mathematical formula for calculating the optimal fraction of capital to risk on each trade to maximize long\-term account growth, given a known edge\.

__Lagging Indicator__

A metric that confirms a trend after it has already formed, such as a season win\-loss record or points\-per\-game average\.

__Leading Indicator__

A metric that attempts to predict future performance based on current conditions, such as pre\-game injury status or matchup efficiency ratings\.

__MACD__

Moving Average Convergence Divergence\. A momentum indicator that measures the relationship between two exponential moving averages to identify trend direction and strength\.

__Mean Reversion__

A trading approach that bets on a price that has moved significantly away from its average will return toward that average\.

__Momentum Trading__

A trading approach that bets on a strong directional price move will continue, based on the principle that momentum tends to persist in the short term\.

__OCO \(One\-Cancels\-Other\)__

An order pair where the execution of one order automatically cancels the other, used in bracket orders to link stop\-losses and profit targets\.

__Order Book__

The live record of all pending buy and sell orders at every price level, showing the depth of buying and selling interest in the market\.

__Process Failure__

A trading loss attributable to a deviation from established process, such as moving a stop\-loss or oversizing based on emotion\.

__RSI \(Relative Strength Index\)__

A momentum oscillator measuring the speed and magnitude of recent price changes on a scale of 0 to 100\.

__Scaling In__

Entering a position in multiple tranches rather than all at once, adding size as the trade confirms your thesis\.

__Scaling Out__

Exiting a position in multiple tranches rather than all at once, capturing gains at different price levels\.

__Second\-Order Thinking__

Analyzing not just what will happen, but what the market believes will happen, and where those beliefs diverge from reality\.

__Shooting Star__

A bearish reversal candlestick with a small body near the bottom and a long upper wick, indicating sellers recovered control after an intraperiod advance\.

__Spread Spike__

A sudden, sharp widening of the bid\-ask spread following a major game event or new information, reflecting rapid repricing and reduced liquidity\.

__Trailing Stop__

A stop\-loss that moves with the price as it goes in your favor, locking in gains while still allowing the position room to continue running\.

__Variance__

Unpredictable fluctuation in outcomes resulting from random game events rather than from process errors\. Normal variance produces losing trades even on correctly identified setups\.

__Wedge__

A chart pattern where converging trend lines squeeze price into a tighter range before a directional breakout\.
